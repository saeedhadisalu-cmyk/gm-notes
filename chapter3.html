Chapter Introduction

Dynamics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of objects and the forces that produce or change their motion.

In this chapter, we study force, momentum, Newton's laws of motion, circular motion and friction.

Remember for Exam: Learn definitions, SI units, formulas, laws and their daily-life examples.

1. Force

Definition: Force is a push or pull that can change or try to change the state of rest or motion of an object.

Effects of Force

  • It can move a stationary object.
  • It can stop a moving object.
  • It can change the speed of an object.
  • It can change the direction of motion.
  • It can change the shape of an object.

SI Unit: Newton (N)

Example: Pushing a trolley or kicking a football.

Exam Point: Force is a vector quantity because it has magnitude and direction.

2. Linear Momentum

Definition: Linear momentum is the quantity of motion possessed by a moving body. It is equal to the product of mass and velocity.

Momentum: p = mv

Where:

  • p = Momentum
  • m = Mass of object
  • v = Velocity of object

SI Unit: kg m/s

Nature: Momentum is a vector quantity. Its direction is the same as the direction of velocity.

Example

A body has a mass of 2 kg and velocity of 3 m/s.

p = mv = 2 × 3

Answer: 6 kg m/s

Remember: Greater mass or greater velocity produces greater momentum.

3. Momentum in Terms of Force

According to Newton's second law, force is equal to the rate of change of momentum.

F = (Final momentum − Initial momentum) / Time

In symbols:

F = Δp / Δt

For constant mass:

F = m(v − u) / t

Where u is initial velocity, v is final velocity and t is time.

SI Unit of Force: Newton (N)

4. Safety Devices

Safety devices are used to reduce the force and injuries during accidents.

Examples

  • Seat belts: They help prevent passengers from moving forward suddenly during a collision.
  • Airbags: They increase the time of impact and reduce the force on passengers.
  • Helmets: They protect the head by reducing the effect of impact.
  • Safety padding: Soft materials help increase the time of impact.
Exam Point: Increasing the time of impact reduces the average force for the same change in momentum.

5. Law of Conservation of Momentum

Statement: The total momentum of an isolated system remains constant if no external force acts on it.

Total momentum before collision = Total momentum after collision

For two bodies:

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Where:

  • m₁ and m₂ = Masses of two bodies
  • u₁ and u₂ = Initial velocities
  • v₁ and v₂ = Final velocities

Example

When two billiard balls collide, momentum is transferred between them. The total momentum of the system remains constant if external forces are negligible.

Important for Exam: Learn the statement and mathematical formula of this law.

6. Newton's Laws of Motion

Sir Isaac Newton presented three important laws of motion. These laws explain the relationship between force and motion.

7. Newton's First Law of Motion

Statement: A body remains at rest or continues moving with uniform velocity in a straight line unless an external unbalanced force acts on it.

This law is also called the Law of Inertia.

Examples

  • A book remains at rest on a table until someone moves it.
  • Passengers move forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.
  • A football remains at rest until it is kicked.
Exam Point: Newton's first law explains inertia.

8. Inertia

Definition: Inertia is the property of a body by which it resists any change in its state of rest or uniform motion.

In simple words, an object does not change its motion easily by itself.

Example

When a bus starts suddenly, passengers tend to fall backward because their bodies tend to remain at rest.

Types of Inertia

  • Inertia of rest: Resistance to a change from rest.
  • Inertia of motion: Resistance to a change in motion.
  • Inertia of direction: Resistance to a change in direction.
Remember: Mass is a measure of inertia. Greater mass means greater inertia.

9. Newton's Second Law of Motion

Statement: The acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass.

For constant mass:

F = ma

Where:

  • F = Net force in Newton (N)
  • m = Mass in kilogram (kg)
  • a = Acceleration in m/s²

Explanation

  • If force increases, acceleration increases.
  • If mass increases while force remains constant, acceleration decreases.

Example

A force of 10 N acts on a body of mass 2 kg. Find acceleration.

F = ma

a = F/m = 10/2

Answer: 5 m/s²

Important: One Newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² in a mass of 1 kg.

10. Newton's Third Law of Motion

Statement: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

When one body exerts a force on another body, the second body exerts an equal force in the opposite direction on the first body.

F(A on B) = − F(B on A)

Examples

  • Walking: Our foot pushes the ground backward, and the ground pushes us forward.
  • Swimming: A swimmer pushes water backward, and water pushes the swimmer forward.
  • Rocket motion: Gases move backward, and the rocket moves forward.
  • Recoil: A moving object can produce an opposite reaction on the object from which it moves.
Exam Point: Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction and act on different bodies.

11. Mass and Weight

Mass

Definition: Mass is the quantity of matter present in a body. It is also a measure of inertia.

  • SI unit: kilogram (kg)
  • Mass remains constant everywhere.
  • Mass is a scalar quantity.

Weight

Definition: Weight is the gravitational force acting on a body.

W = mg

Where W = Weight, m = Mass and g = Acceleration due to gravity.

  • SI unit: Newton (N)
  • Weight can change from place to place.
  • Weight is a vector quantity.

12. Uniform Circular Motion

Definition: The motion of an object in a circular path with constant speed is called uniform circular motion.

In this motion, speed remains constant, but velocity changes because the direction of motion changes continuously.

Examples

  • Motion of a fan blade.
  • Motion of the hands of a clock.
  • A satellite moving in a circular orbit.
Remember: Uniform circular motion is accelerated motion because the direction of velocity changes continuously.

13. Centripetal Force

Definition: The force that acts towards the centre of a circular path and keeps an object moving in that path is called centripetal force.

F = mv²/r

Where:

  • m = Mass of object (kg)
  • v = Speed (m/s)
  • r = Radius of circular path (m)

Direction: Always towards the centre of the circle.

Examples

  • Gravitational force keeps planets in orbit.
  • Tension in a string keeps a stone moving in a circle.
  • Friction helps a car turn on a level road.

14. Centrifugal Force

Definition: Centrifugal force is the apparent outward force experienced by an object when observed from a rotating frame of reference.

It appears to act away from the centre of circular motion.

Examples

  • Passengers feel pushed outward when a car turns.
  • Water moves outward in a rotating container.
Difference: Centripetal force acts towards the centre, while apparent centrifugal force acts outward in a rotating frame.

15. Applications of Centrifugal Effect

A. Banking of Roads

On a curved road, the outer edge is raised above the inner edge. This is called banking of roads.

Banking helps vehicles turn safely by providing the required centripetal force through the road's normal force and friction, depending on conditions.

B. Cream Separator

A cream separator is a machine used to separate cream from milk.

When milk rotates rapidly, its components separate because of their different densities. The denser part moves farther outward, while the lighter cream stays closer to the axis.

Exam Point: Learn the purpose and working principle of both applications.

16. Friction

Definition: Friction is a force that opposes the relative motion or tendency of motion between two surfaces in contact.

Friction acts parallel to the surfaces in contact and opposite to the direction of relative motion or its tendency.

Types of Friction

A. Static Friction

Static friction acts between surfaces when there is no relative motion between them. It prevents an object from starting to move.

Example: Pushing a heavy box that does not move.

B. Kinetic Friction

Kinetic friction acts when two surfaces are moving relative to each other.

Example: A box sliding across a floor.

C. Sliding Friction

Sliding friction acts when one surface slides over another surface.

Example: A book sliding on a table.

D. Rolling Friction

Rolling friction acts when an object rolls over a surface.

Example: A wheel rolling on a road.

Important: Rolling friction is usually smaller than sliding friction for the same surfaces and conditions.

17. Advantages of Friction

  • It helps us walk without slipping.
  • It helps vehicles stop when brakes are applied.
  • It helps us write with a pencil.
  • It allows us to hold objects firmly.
  • It helps tyres grip the road.

18. Disadvantages of Friction

  • It causes wear and tear of machine parts.
  • It produces unwanted heat.
  • It wastes energy.
  • It reduces the efficiency of machines.
  • It makes moving objects harder.

19. Methods to Reduce Friction

  • Using lubricants such as oil and grease.
  • Using ball bearings.
  • Polishing surfaces.
  • Using wheels instead of sliding surfaces.

22. Important MCQs

1. SI unit of momentum is:

(a) Newton   (b) kg m/s   (c) Joule   (d) Watt

Answer: (b) kg m/s

2. Newton's first law is also called:

(a) Law of force   (b) Law of inertia   (c) Law of energy

Answer: (b) Law of inertia

3. The formula of Newton's second law is:

(a) F = ma   (b) p = mv   (c) W = mg

Answer: (a) F = ma

4. Action and reaction are:

(a) Equal and opposite   (b) Unequal   (c) Same direction

Answer: (a) Equal and opposite

5. SI unit of weight is:

(a) kg   (b) Newton   (c) metre

Answer: (b) Newton

6. Centripetal force acts:

(a) Away from centre   (b) Towards centre   (c) Upward

Answer: (b) Towards centre

7. Which friction is usually the smallest?

(a) Sliding   (b) Rolling   (c) Static

Answer: (b) Rolling

8. The law of conservation of momentum applies to:

(a) An isolated system   (b) Every open system without conditions

Answer: (a) An isolated system

9. A cream separator works using:

(a) Rotation   (b) Magnetism   (c) Reflection

Answer: (a) Rotation

10. The measure of inertia is:

(a) Speed   (b) Mass   (c) Distance

Answer: (b) Mass

  • Weight = mg.
  • Centripetal force = Towards centre.
  • Centrifugal force = Apparent outward force in a rotating frame.
  • Friction = Opposes relative motion.
  • Rolling friction is usually less than sliding friction.
