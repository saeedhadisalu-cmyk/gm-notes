1. Introduction to Chemistry

What is Chemistry?

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the composition, structure, properties and changes of matter.

Importance of Chemistry

  • It helps in the preparation of medicines.
  • It is used in agriculture to make fertilizers.
  • It helps in the production of soaps and detergents.
  • It is used in food preservation.
  • It helps in the production of plastics, glass and paints.
  • It helps us understand changes in matter.

1.2 Branches of Chemistry

1. Physical Chemistry

It deals with the physical properties of matter and the changes that occur during chemical reactions.

2. Organic Chemistry

It deals mainly with hydrocarbons and their derivatives. Example: petrol, plastics and natural gas.

3. Inorganic Chemistry

It deals with elements and their compounds, generally excluding organic compounds. Example: salts, minerals and metals.

4. Biochemistry

It deals with chemical substances and processes in living organisms. Example: proteins, carbohydrates and fats.

5. Industrial Chemistry

It deals with the production of chemicals on a large scale in industries.

6. Nuclear Chemistry

It deals with radioactivity and changes in atomic nuclei.

7. Environmental Chemistry

It studies chemicals and their effects on the environment.

8. Analytical Chemistry

It deals with the identification and measurement of substances present in a sample.

9. Medicinal Chemistry

It deals with the development and study of chemical substances used in medicines.

10. Quantum Chemistry

It applies quantum mechanics to understand chemical systems.

11. Green Chemistry

It focuses on designing chemical products and processes that reduce harmful substances and waste.

1.3 Basic Definitions

1. Matter

Anything that has mass and occupies space is called matter.

Examples: Air, water, wood and stone.

2. Atom

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that takes part in chemical reactions.

3. Molecule

A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together.

Examples: H₂, O₂ and H₂O.

4. Substance

A pure form of matter having a definite composition and characteristic properties is called a substance.

5. Element

An element is a pure substance made up of only one type of atom.

Examples: Hydrogen, oxygen, iron and carbon.

6. Symbol

A symbol is a short form used to represent an element.

Examples: Hydrogen = H, Oxygen = O, Sodium = Na.

Rules for Writing Symbols

  • A symbol usually has one or two letters.
  • The first letter is always capital.
  • The second letter, if present, is always small.

7. Valency

The combining capacity of an atom of an element is called valency.

8. Chemical Formula

A chemical formula represents the elements and the number of their atoms present in a substance.

Examples: H₂O, CO₂ and NH₃.

9. Compound

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more elements combine chemically in a fixed ratio.

Examples: Water (H₂O) and sodium chloride (NaCl).

10. Mixture

A mixture is formed when two or more substances are mixed physically in any proportion.

Examples: Air, salt water and soil.

Difference Between Element, Compound and Mixture

Element Compound Mixture
Contains one type of atom. Contains two or more elements chemically combined. Contains two or more substances physically mixed.
Cannot be broken into simpler substances by ordinary chemical methods. Can be separated into elements by chemical methods. Can usually be separated by physical methods.
Represented by a symbol. Represented by a formula. Has no fixed chemical formula.
Example: Iron. Example: Water. Example: Air.

Types of Mixtures

Homogeneous Mixture: A mixture with uniform composition throughout. Example: salt water.

Heterogeneous Mixture: A mixture with non-uniform composition. Example: sand and water.

Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atomic Number (Z)

The number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom is called its atomic number.

Atomic Number (Z) = Number of Protons

Mass Number (A)

The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom is called its mass number.

Mass Number (A) = Protons + Neutrons
Number of Neutrons = A − Z
Example:
An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons.
Atomic number = 11
Mass number = 11 + 12 = 23

Atomic Mass Unit (amu)

Atomic mass unit is a unit used to express the masses of atoms and molecules.

1 amu ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g

Relative Atomic Mass

Relative atomic mass is the average mass of an atom of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Empirical Formula and Molecular Formula

Empirical Formula

The formula that shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of different elements in a compound is called the empirical formula.

Example: Benzene has empirical formula CH.

Molecular Formula

The formula that shows the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule is called the molecular formula.

Example: Benzene has molecular formula C₆H₆.

Compound Empirical Formula Molecular Formula
Benzene CH C₆H₆
Glucose CH₂O C₆H₁₂O₆
Hydrogen Peroxide HO H₂O₂
Molecular Formula = (Empirical Formula) × n

Here, n is a whole number.

Molecular Mass and Formula Mass

Molecular Mass

The sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule is called molecular mass.

Example: Find molecular mass of H₂O.
H = 1 amu
O = 16 amu

Molecular mass = 2(1) + 16
Answer = 18 amu

Formula Mass

The sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in a formula unit of an ionic compound is called formula mass.

Example: Find formula mass of NaCl.
Na = 23 amu
Cl = 35.5 amu

Formula mass = 23 + 35.5
Answer = 58.5 amu

1.4 Chemical Species

A chemical species is a chemical entity such as an atom, ion or molecule.

1. Cation

A positively charged ion formed when an atom loses electrons is called a cation.

Examples: Na⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺.

2. Anion

A negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains electrons is called an anion.

Examples: Cl⁻ and O²⁻.

3. Molecular Ion

A charged group of atoms is called a molecular ion.

Examples: NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻.

4. Free Radical

A free radical is an atom or group of atoms containing one or more unpaired electrons.

Examples: H• and Cl•.

1.5 Chemical Equation

A chemical equation is a short way of representing a chemical reaction by using symbols and chemical formulae.

Reactants

The substances that take part in a chemical reaction are called reactants. They are written on the left side.

Products

The new substances formed in a chemical reaction are called products. They are written on the right side.

Reactants → Products

Example

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Here, hydrogen and oxygen are reactants, while water is the product.

Symbols Used in Chemical Equations

Symbol Meaning
(s) Solid
(l) Liquid
(g) Gas
(aq) Aqueous solution
→ Produces or forms

Balancing Chemical Equations

A chemical equation is balanced when the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides.

It follows the Law of Conservation of Mass.

Steps to Balance an Equation

  1. Write the correct formulae of reactants and products.
  2. Count the atoms on both sides.
  3. Add suitable coefficients before the formulae.
  4. Check that all atoms are equal on both sides.
Example: Balance the equation.

Unbalanced:
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Balanced:
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Remember: Change coefficients to balance an equation. Do not change the small numbers (subscripts) in chemical formulae.

1.6 Mole and Avogadro's Number

Gram Atomic Mass

The atomic mass of an element expressed in grams is called gram atomic mass.

Example: 1 mole of carbon atoms = 12 g.

Gram Molecular Mass

The molecular mass of a substance expressed in grams is called gram molecular mass.

Example: 1 mole of water = 18 g.

Gram Formula Mass

The formula mass of an ionic compound expressed in grams is called gram formula mass.

Example: 1 mole of NaCl = 58.5 g.

What is a Mole?

A mole is the amount of substance that contains 6.02 × 10²³ elementary particles.

1 Mole = 6.02 × 10²³ Particles

Avogadro's Number

The number of particles present in one mole of a substance is called Avogadro's number.

Nₐ = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

Important Mole Formulae

Number of Moles = Mass / Molar Mass
Mass = Number of Moles × Molar Mass
Number of Particles = Moles × 6.02 × 10²³
Moles = Number of Particles / 6.02 × 10²³

1.7 Chemical Calculations

1. Mole-Mass Calculation

Question: Calculate the number of moles in 40 g of sodium (Na).

Given:
Mass = 40 g
Molar mass of Na = 23 g/mol

Formula:
Moles = Mass / Molar Mass

Solution:
Moles = 40 / 23
Answer = 1.74 mol (approximately)

2. Calculate Mass

Question: Find the mass of 4 moles of CO₂.

Molar mass of CO₂ = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44 g/mol
Mass = Moles × Molar Mass
Mass = 4 × 44
Answer = 176 g

3. Mole-Particle Calculation

Question: How many molecules are present in 2 moles of water?

Formula:
Particles = Moles × Avogadro's Number

= 2 × 6.02 × 10²³
Answer = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

4. Mole-Volume Calculation

At STP, one mole of an ideal gas occupies approximately 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L).

Gas Volume at STP = Moles × 22.4 L
Question: Find the volume of 2 moles of a gas at STP.

Volume = 2 × 22.4
Answer = 44.8 L
STP in this textbook means 0°C temperature and approximately 1 atmosphere pressure.
