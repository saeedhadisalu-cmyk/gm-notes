1. Gravitation

Gravitation is the force of attraction between any two objects that have mass.

Example: The Earth attracts objects towards its center. That is why objects fall towards the ground.

2. Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force. This force is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers.

F = G(m₁ × m₂) / r²
  • F = Gravitational force (N)
  • G = Universal gravitational constant
  • m₁ and m₂ = Masses of two objects (kg)
  • r = Distance between their centers (m)

Key Points

  1. Every object attracts every other object.
  2. Gravitational force is always attractive.
  3. Greater mass produces greater gravitational force.
  4. Greater distance produces smaller gravitational force.
  5. The force acts along the line joining the centers.
  6. Gravitational force follows the inverse-square law.
Example: Two objects have masses of 2 kg and 3 kg. Their distance is 2 m. Find the gravitational force.
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²
F = G(m₁m₂)/r²
F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2 × 3)/2²
Answer: F = 1.0005 × 10⁻¹⁰ N

3. Newton's Law of Gravitation and Third Law of Motion

Newton's third law states that for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

  • The Earth attracts the Moon.
  • The Moon also attracts the Earth.
  • Both forces have equal magnitude.
  • Both forces act in opposite directions.
  • These forces act on different objects.

4. Newton's Three Laws of Motion (5 Lines)

  1. First Law: An object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless an external force acts on it.
  2. Second Law: The force acting on an object is equal to its mass multiplied by acceleration.
  3. Formula: F = ma.
  4. Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  5. Example: When we push a wall, the wall pushes us back with an equal and opposite force.

5. Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

The universal gravitational constant is the constant used in Newton's law of universal gravitation.

G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²

SI Unit: Nm²/kg²

It has the same value everywhere in the universe.

6. Gravitational Field

A gravitational field is the region around an object in which another object experiences gravitational force.

Example: The Earth has a gravitational field around it. Objects in this field experience an attractive force.

g = F / m
  • g = Gravitational field strength
  • F = Gravitational force
  • m = Mass of object

SI Unit: N/kg

Near Earth's surface, g = 9.8 m/s².

7. Mass of the Earth

The mass of the Earth is the total amount of matter present in the Earth.

M = gR² / G

Where M is the mass of Earth, R is its radius, g is acceleration due to gravity and G is the gravitational constant.

Values:
g = 9.8 m/s²
R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg²
Result: M ≈ 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg

8. Artificial Satellites

An artificial satellite is a human-made object that revolves around the Earth or another celestial body in an orbit.

Examples

  • Communication satellites
  • Weather satellites
  • Navigation satellites
  • Scientific research satellites

9. Gravitation and Motion of Satellites

The gravitational force of the Earth provides the centripetal force required to keep a satellite moving in its orbit.

Gravitational force:

F = GMm / r²

Centripetal force:

F = mv² / r

By comparing both equations:

GMm/r² = mv²/r
v = √(GM/r)

This is the orbital speed of a satellite in a circular orbit.

10. Motion of an Artificial Satellite Around Earth

  • A satellite moves along an orbit around the Earth.
  • Gravity acts towards the center of the Earth.
  • Gravity provides the centripetal force.
  • In a circular orbit, its speed remains constant.
  • It continuously revolves around the Earth.

11. Types of Satellites

Natural Satellites

These are naturally occurring objects that revolve around planets.

Example: The Moon.

Artificial Satellites

These are human-made objects placed into orbit.

Examples: Communication and weather satellites.

Common Artificial Satellite Orbits

  • Geostationary Orbit: A satellite appears fixed above the same point on Earth.
  • Polar Orbit: A satellite passes over or near the Earth's poles.
  • Low Earth Orbit (LEO): A satellite moves relatively close to Earth.

12. Mass of a Satellite

Mass is the amount of matter present in a satellite.

  • Mass is measured in kilograms (kg).
  • Mass does not change with location.
  • Weight can change depending on gravitational field strength.
W = mg

W = Weight, m = Mass, g = Gravitational acceleration.

13. Orbital Velocity

Orbital velocity is the speed required by a satellite to remain in its orbit around the Earth.

v = √(GM/r)

G = Gravitational constant, M = Mass of Earth, r = Distance from Earth's center to satellite.

Example:
GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s²
r = 7.0 × 10⁶ m
v = √[(3.986 × 10¹⁴)/(7.0 × 10⁶)]
Answer: v ≈ 7548 m/s

14. Critical Velocity

Critical velocity is the minimum horizontal velocity required by an object to move in a circular orbit around the Earth at a given distance from its center.

v₍c₎ = √(GM/r)

At the Earth's surface, the ideal orbital value is approximately:

v₍c₎ ≈ 7.9 km/s

This is an ideal value. Actual satellites orbit above the atmosphere.

15. Time Period of a Satellite

The time period is the time taken by a satellite to complete one revolution around the Earth.

T = 2π√(r³/GM)

T is the time period in seconds.

16. Important Formula Revision

  • Newton's gravitation law: F = Gm₁m₂/r²
  • Gravitational field strength: g = F/m
  • Weight: W = mg
  • Mass of Earth: M = gR²/G
  • Orbital velocity: v = √(GM/r)
  • Critical velocity: v₍c₎ = √(GM/r)
  • Time period: T = 2π√(r³/GM)
  • Centripetal force: F = mv²/r
