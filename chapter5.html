Chapter 5: Forces and Matter

Class 9 Physics | Simple English Notes

1. Introduction to Forces

A force is a push or pull that can change the state of motion, direction, speed or shape of an object.

Examples of Force

  • Pushing a shopping cart.
  • Pulling a door to open it.
  • Kicking a football.
  • Lifting a school bag.

Effects of Force

  • Force can start the motion of an object.
  • Force can stop a moving object.
  • Force can change the speed of an object.
  • Force can change the direction of motion.
  • Force can change the shape or size of an object.
SI Unit of Force = Newton (N)

Force is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction.

2. Forces Acting on Solids

When a force acts on a solid object, it may change its shape, size or motion.

Types of Deformation

  • Stretching: Pulling an object and making it longer.
  • Compression: Pressing an object and making it shorter.
  • Bending: Applying force to curve an object.
  • Twisting: Applying a turning force to an object.

For example, pressing a sponge changes its shape. Pulling a spring makes it longer.

Elastic Deformation

When an object returns to its original shape after removing the force, the deformation is called elastic deformation.

Plastic Deformation

When an object does not return completely to its original shape after removing the force, the deformation is called plastic deformation.

3. Stretching Springs

A spring is an elastic object that can be stretched or compressed by applying a force.

When a load is attached to a spring, the spring becomes longer. When the load is removed, the spring may return to its original length if its elastic limit has not been exceeded.

Important Terms

  • Original Length: Length of a spring before applying a load.
  • Final Length: Length of a spring after applying a load.
  • Extension: Increase in the length of a spring.
  • Load: The force applied to a spring, usually due to a hanging object.
Extension = Final Length − Original Length

Symbolically:

x = L − L₀

Here, x = extension, L = final length and L₀ = original length.

4. Relationship Between Load and Extension

When a load is added to a spring, its extension increases. Within the elastic limit, the extension is directly proportional to the applied force.

5. Hooke's Law

Hooke's Law states that the extension of an elastic spring is directly proportional to the applied force, provided that the elastic limit is not exceeded.

F = kx

Where:

  • F = Applied force in Newton (N)
  • k = Spring constant in N/m
  • x = Extension in metres (m)

Spring Constant

The spring constant tells us how stiff a spring is. A larger spring constant means that more force is needed to produce the same extension.

k = F / x

Elastic Limit

The maximum limit up to which a spring returns to its original shape after removing the force is called its elastic limit.

Example: A spring is stretched by 0.05 m when a force of 10 N is applied. Find its spring constant.

Given: F = 10 N, x = 0.05 m
Formula: k = F / x
k = 10 / 0.05
Answer: k = 200 N/m

6. Pressure

Pressure is the force acting normally (perpendicularly) per unit area of a surface.

Pressure = Force / Area
P = F / A

SI unit of pressure is Pascal (Pa).

1 Pascal = 1 Newton / 1 square metre
1 Pa = 1 N/m²

Pressure is a scalar quantity.

How to Calculate Pressure

Example: A force of 100 N acts on an area of 2 m². Find the pressure.

Given: F = 100 N, A = 2 m²
Formula: P = F / A
P = 100 / 2
Answer: P = 50 Pa

7. High Pressure and Low Pressure

Pressure depends on force and area.

High Pressure

For the same force, pressure is high when the area is small.

Examples:

  • A sharp knife cuts easily because its edge has a small area.
  • A pointed nail enters wood easily because its tip has a small area.

Low Pressure

For the same force, pressure is low when the area is large.

Examples:

  • Wide tyres help heavy vehicles spread their weight over a larger area.
  • Wide school bag straps spread the force over a larger area and feel more comfortable.
Small Area = High Pressure
Large Area = Low Pressure

8. Pressure in Fluids

Fluids are substances that can flow. Liquids and gases are both fluids.

Fluids exert pressure on the surfaces and objects around them. Liquid pressure acts in all directions.

Pressure in Liquids

Liquid pressure increases with depth. This means that pressure is greater at the bottom of a liquid container than near its surface.

P = ρgh

Where:

  • P = Liquid pressure (Pa)
  • ρ = Density of liquid (kg/m³)
  • g = Gravitational acceleration (m/s²)
  • h = Depth of liquid (m)
Example: Calculate the pressure due to water at a depth of 2 m.

Given: ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s², h = 2 m
Formula: P = ρgh
P = 1000 × 10 × 2
Answer: P = 20,000 Pa

9. Factors Affecting Pressure in Fluids

Liquid pressure depends mainly on the following factors:

1. Depth

Pressure increases when depth increases.

2. Density

A denser liquid produces more pressure at the same depth.

3. Gravitational Acceleration

Liquid pressure also depends on the gravitational acceleration.

P = ρgh

Important: At the same depth in the same liquid, pressure is equal. The shape of the container does not affect the pressure at that depth.

10. Atmospheric Pressure

The air surrounding the Earth has weight and exerts pressure. This pressure is called atmospheric pressure.

Atmospheric pressure decreases as altitude increases.

Example: A drinking straw works because of the pressure difference created when we suck air out of the straw.

Standard Atmospheric Pressure ≈ 101,325 Pa

11. Pascal's Law

Pascal's Law states that when pressure is applied to an enclosed fluid, the pressure change is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions throughout the fluid and to the walls of its container.

Applications of Pascal's Law

  • Hydraulic press
  • Hydraulic lift
  • Hydraulic brakes
  • Hydraulic jack

12. Hydraulic Machines

Hydraulic machines use liquid pressure to transmit force from one place to another. They work on Pascal's Law.

Hydraulic Press

A hydraulic press uses liquid pressure to produce a large force. It is used for pressing and shaping materials.

Hydraulic Lift

A hydraulic lift is used to raise heavy objects, such as cars, by applying a smaller force to a smaller piston.

Hydraulic Brakes

Hydraulic brakes use liquid pressure to transfer the force from the brake pedal to the brakes at the wheels.

Hydraulic Machine Formula

F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂

Where:

  • F₁ = Force on the small piston
  • A₁ = Area of the small piston
  • F₂ = Force on the large piston
  • A₂ = Area of the large piston
Example: A force of 20 N is applied to a small piston of area 0.01 m². The large piston has an area of 0.1 m². Find the output force.

Given: F₁ = 20 N, A₁ = 0.01 m², A₂ = 0.1 m²
Formula: F₂ = (F₁ × A₂) / A₁
F₂ = (20 × 0.1) / 0.01
Answer: F₂ = 200 N

15. Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs)

  1. The SI unit of force is:
    A) Pascal   B) Newton   C) Joule   D) Watt
    Answer: B) Newton

  2. Pressure is equal to:
    A) Force × Area   B) Force / Area
    C) Area / Force   D) Mass × Speed
    Answer: B) Force / Area

  3. The SI unit of pressure is:
    A) Newton   B) Joule   C) Pascal   D) Metre
    Answer: C) Pascal

  4. Hooke's Law is valid within the:
    A) Elastic limit   B) Melting point
    C) Boiling point   D) Freezing point
    Answer: A) Elastic limit

  5. Liquid pressure increases with:
    A) Height above liquid   B) Depth
    C) Container color   D) Container shape
    Answer: B) Depth

  6. Pascal's Law is used in:
    A) Hydraulic lift   B) Thermometer
    C) Telescope   D) Electric bulb
    Answer: A) Hydraulic lift

  7. A sharp knife produces:
    A) Low pressure   B) High pressure
    C) Zero pressure   D) No force
    Answer: B) High pressure

  8. The spring constant is measured in:
    A) N/m   B) N/m²   C) kg/m³   D) Pa
    Answer: A) N/m
