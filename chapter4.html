Chapter 4: Turning Effect of Forces

Class 9 Physics | Simple English Notes

← Back to Physics

Chapter Introduction

Turning Effect of Forces is the study of how forces can turn objects around a fixed point or axis. It also explains how forces keep objects in equilibrium.

Examples include opening a door, using a spanner, balancing a seesaw and turning a steering wheel.

Exam Tip: Learn definitions, formulas, SI units, differences and conditions of equilibrium.

1. Like and Unlike Parallel Forces

Parallel Forces

Forces whose lines of action are parallel to each other are called parallel forces.

A. Like Parallel Forces

Parallel forces acting in the same direction are called like parallel forces.

Example: Two people pushing a box in the same direction.

Resultant = F₁ + F₂

B. Unlike Parallel Forces

Parallel forces acting in opposite directions are called unlike parallel forces.

Example: Two people pushing a box in opposite directions.

Resultant = Larger force − Smaller force

The resultant acts in the direction of the larger force.

Remember: Like forces act in the same direction; unlike forces act in opposite directions.

2. Addition of Forces

Definition: Addition of forces means finding one single force that has the same effect as two or more forces acting together. This single force is called the resultant force.

Forces in the Same Direction

R = F₁ + F₂

Forces in Opposite Directions

R = |F₁ − F₂|

The resultant acts in the direction of the larger force.

Example

Two forces of 10 N and 5 N act in the same direction.

R = 10 + 5 = 15 N

3. Head-to-Tail Rule

Definition: The head-to-tail rule is a graphical method used to add vectors. The tail of the second vector is placed at the head of the first vector.

To find the resultant, draw a line from the tail of the first vector to the head of the last vector.

Steps

  1. Draw the first vector with its correct direction and length.
  2. Place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first vector.
  3. Continue the same process for other vectors.
  4. Draw the resultant from the starting point to the final endpoint.
Exam Point: The head-to-tail rule is used for the addition of vector quantities, including force.

4. Resolution of Forces

Definition: Resolution of a force is the process of splitting one force into two or more components in different directions.

A force can be resolved into two perpendicular components: horizontal and vertical.

If a force F makes an angle θ with the horizontal:

Horizontal component: Fₓ = F cos θ
Vertical component: Fᵧ = F sin θ

Here, θ is measured from the horizontal direction.

Remember: Use cosine for the horizontal component and sine for the vertical component when the angle is measured from the horizontal.

5. Determination of Force from Its Perpendicular Components

If the horizontal and vertical components of a force are known, the original force can be found using Pythagoras' theorem.

F = √(Fₓ² + Fᵧ²)

The direction of the force is:

tan θ = Fᵧ / Fₓ

Therefore:

θ = tan⁻¹(Fᵧ / Fₓ)

Example

A force has horizontal component 3 N and vertical component 4 N. Find the resultant force.

F = √(3² + 4²)

F = √(9 + 16) = √25

Answer: 5 N

6. Torque or Moment of Force

Definition: Torque is the turning effect of a force around a fixed point or axis.

In simple words, torque tells us how strongly a force can turn an object.

Examples: Opening a door, turning a tap and using a spanner.

SI Unit: Newton metre (N m).

Symbol: τ (tau).

Torque depends on the applied force, the distance from the pivot and the angle between the force and the position vector.

Important: A force applied farther from the pivot usually produces a greater turning effect.

7. Principle of Moments

Definition: For a body in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about a point is equal to the sum of anticlockwise moments about the same point.

Clockwise moments = Anticlockwise moments

Or:

Στ = 0

Example

A seesaw is balanced when the clockwise turning effect is equal to the anticlockwise turning effect.

Exam Tip: Remember the words clockwise and anticlockwise in the principle of moments.

8. Centre of Mass

Definition: The centre of mass is the point at which the whole mass of a body can be considered to be concentrated.

In simple words, it is the average position of the mass of an object.

Example: The centre of mass of a uniform ruler is at its midpoint.

Centre of Mass of Regular Objects

  • Uniform rod: At its midpoint.
  • Rectangle or square lamina: At the intersection of its diagonals.
  • Uniform circular disc: At its geometric centre.
  • Uniform sphere: At its centre.

9. Centre of Gravity

Definition: The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole weight of a body appears to act.

In simple words, it is the point where the body's total weight may be considered to act.

Example: For a uniform ruler, the centre of gravity is at its midpoint.

Finding the Centre of Gravity of an Irregular Object

  1. Make two or more small holes near the edge of the object.
  2. Suspend the object freely from one hole.
  3. Hang a plumb line from the same point.
  4. Draw the vertical line along the plumb line.
  5. Repeat from another hole.
  6. The intersection of the lines gives the centre of gravity.
Remember: In a uniform gravitational field, the centre of mass and centre of gravity are at the same point.

10. Equilibrium

Definition: A body is in equilibrium when the resultant force and the resultant torque acting on it are zero.

In simple words, all the forces and turning effects are balanced.

Types of Equilibrium

A. Static Equilibrium

A body is in static equilibrium when it remains at rest and all forces and torques are balanced.

Example: A book resting on a table.

B. Dynamic Equilibrium

A body is in dynamic equilibrium when it moves with constant velocity and all forces and torques are balanced.

Example: A car moving in a straight line with constant velocity, when the net force and net torque are zero.

Exam Point: Static equilibrium means at rest; dynamic equilibrium means moving with constant velocity.

11. Conditions for Equilibrium

For a rigid body to be in complete equilibrium, two main conditions must be satisfied.

First Condition of Equilibrium

The vector sum of all forces acting on a body must be zero.

ΣFₓ = 0
ΣFᵧ = 0

This means the body has no linear acceleration.

Second Condition of Equilibrium

The sum of all clockwise and anticlockwise torques about any point must be balanced.

Στ = 0

This means the body has no angular acceleration.

Very Important: First condition balances forces; second condition balances turning effects.

12. States of Equilibrium

A. Stable Equilibrium

A body is in stable equilibrium if it returns to its original position after a small displacement.

Example: A ball at the bottom of a bowl.

B. Unstable Equilibrium

A body is in unstable equilibrium if it moves farther away from its original position after a small displacement.

Example: A ball balanced on top of a hill.

C. Neutral Equilibrium

A body is in neutral equilibrium if it stays in its new position after a small displacement.

Example: A ball on a flat horizontal surface.

13. Stability

Definition: Stability is the ability of a body to return to its original position after a small disturbance.

Factors Affecting Stability

  • Low centre of gravity: A lower centre of gravity generally increases stability.
  • Wide base: A wider base generally makes an object more stable.

Example: A wide vehicle with a low centre of gravity is generally more stable than a narrow, tall vehicle.

Remember: Low centre of gravity and wide base increase stability.

16. Important MCQs

1. Parallel forces acting in the same direction are called:

(a) Unlike forces (b) Like forces (c) Perpendicular forces

Answer: (b) Like forces

2. The turning effect of a force is called:

(a) Momentum (b) Torque (c) Speed

Answer: (b) Torque

3. The SI unit of torque is:

(a) N m (b) N/s (c) kg m/s

Answer: (a) N m

4. The first condition of equilibrium is:

(a) ΣF = 0 (b) Στ = 0 (c) F = ma

Answer: (a) ΣF = 0

5. The second condition of equilibrium is:

(a) ΣF = 0 (b) Στ = 0 (c) p = mv

Answer: (b) Στ = 0

6. The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole ______ acts.

(a) Mass (b) Weight (c) Speed

Answer: (b) Weight

7. A body that returns to its original position after a small displacement is in:

(a) Stable equilibrium (b) Unstable equilibrium (c) Neutral equilibrium

Answer: (a) Stable equilibrium

8. A ball on a flat horizontal surface is an example of:

(a) Stable (b) Unstable (c) Neutral equilibrium

Answer: (c) Neutral equilibrium

9. A wider base generally makes a body:

(a) More stable (b) Less stable (c) Weightless

Answer: (a) More stable

10. The centre of mass of a uniform rod is at:

(a) One end (b) Its midpoint (c) Outside the rod

Answer: (b) Its midpoint
